If you're determined to license background music for your business the traditional way (direct from rightsholders), here's exactly what that process involves. As a caveat, these steps are more involved than most business owners expect.

## Assess your licensing needs (First Step)

Before contacting anyone, you need to know which PROs own the rights to the specific songs you want to play. This practically means building a list of every artist, album, or playlist you want in rotation. Searching each PRO's public repertoire database (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, and GMR each maintain one) to identify which organization represents which song. Accounting for the fact that individual songs are often split across multiple PROs, since co-writers can be affiliated with different organizations. For a venue playing a genuinely varied, contemporary playlist, this step alone can mean cross-referencing hundreds of tracks against four separate databases.

## Contact the relevant PROs and CMOs (Second Step)

Once you know which organizations cover your target music, you have two options: (1) Get a blanket license from each relevant PRO, covering their entire catalog for a flat fee, the more practical route if you want broad flexibility in what you play. (2) License individual songs one at a time, by contacting the specific artist, songwriter, or publisher directly, usually only realistic for a very small, fixed playlist. Most businesses end up needing blanket licenses from multiple PROs simultaneously to have real freedom over their playlist, which means multiple separate applications, contracts, and fee negotiations running in parallel.

## Understand fees, limits, and stay compliant (Third Step)

Interestingly, licensing is not a one-time transaction. Once licenses are in place, you're responsible for: (a) Fee structures, which vary by PRO and are often based on venue size, capacity, or usage (b) Renewal terms and any changes to what's covered under each license over time (c)Compliance monitoring such as keeping records of what you play and being prepared to demonstrate your licenses are current if a rights organization audits your venue (d) Staying within license limits like a blanket license from one PRO doesn't retroactively cover a song that later turns out to belong to a writer represented elsewhere

## What this actually looks like in practice

Doing this properly, for a venue that wants real playlist flexibility, typically means engaging a lawyer or licensing consultant with music rights experience, negotiating separately with ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, and GMR (and potentially SoundExchange for recording rights), paying upfront fees to each organization before you've played a single song, building and maintaining an internal system to track compliance, usage reporting, and renewals indefinitely, for as long as you're playing music in your venue. None of this is a fast process. Negotiations with multiple rights holders can take months, and for some businesses, years, before every piece is in place.
